3 · Sentiment Analysis

Scoring tone with VADER

NoteBy the end of this section you will be able to
  • Explain how rule-based sentiment analysis works
  • Interpret VADER sentiment scores applied to the interview dataset
  • Critically evaluate what the results reveal and what they miss

What Is Sentiment Analysis?

Sentiment analysis assigns a numerical score to text to indicate whether the language is positive, negative, or neutral. It does not read for meaning; it measures the surface tone of words.

Rule-based sentiment tools work from a lexicon: a dictionary of words pre-scored for emotional valence. When the tool sees a word like honest or trust, it adds to the positive score. When it sees mislead or frustrated, it adds to the negative score. The final score is a sum of these signals, normalized to a standard scale.

TipVADER

VADER (Valence Aware Dictionary and sEntiment Reasoner) was designed for short, informal social media text. It handles:

  • Capitalization (GREAT scores higher than great)
  • Punctuation (great!!! scores higher than great)
  • Degree modifiers (very good > good, barely good < good)
  • Negation (not good flips the polarity)
  • Common slang and emoji

VADER returns four scores per text:

Score Meaning
positive Proportion of tokens with positive valence
negative Proportion of tokens with negative valence
neutral Proportion of neutral tokens
compound Overall score, normalized to −1.0 (most negative) to +1.0 (most positive)

A compound score ≥ +0.05 is classified positive; ≤ −0.05 is negative; between is neutral.

Interpreting the Results

ImportantThe central interpretive question

If 48 of 50 paragraphs score as positive, what does that actually mean?

It could mean the speakers are genuinely positive. But it more likely reveals something about genre. These are interview responses in which influencers are explaining and justifying their professional practices. That genre calls for positive, confident language, even when describing a problem.

VADER was trained on tweets and product reviews, where positive/negative signals are direct and intentional. Interview responses tend to use positively valenced words (honest, trust, values, responsible) to frame what are often complex or ambiguous situations.

The tool cannot distinguish between: - “I believe in transparency” (genuine conviction) - “I always say I believe in transparency” (performative) - “I believe in transparency, but sometimes it’s difficult” (with reservation)

All three would score very similarly.

What the scores do reveal

Even within a uniformly positive distribution, variation matters:

  • Ben-003 is one of the only negative-scoring paragraphs. Read it: Ben is describing other influencers’ unethical behavior. The negative score tracks the critique.
  • Speakers with lower averages (Ben, Gina) tend to use more hedged or critical language than those at the top (Alex, Otto).
  • The width of the box plots for each speaker indicates how consistent their tone is across their responses.

Discussion

  1. Why might professional interview language consistently score as positive even when the content involves criticism?
  2. Does high sentiment mean the speaker is being sincere or polished?
  3. What kind of text would VADER analyze more reliably than these interviews?
  4. If you used a different sentiment tool trained on formal professional speech, would you expect the results to differ? In what direction?

What Sentiment Analysis Adds

Sentiment analysis is most useful when:

  • You have a large corpus where manual reading is impractical
  • The text comes from genres where sentiment is direct (social media, reviews, comment sections)
  • You are comparing sentiment between categories (speakers, time periods, platforms)

For interview data, the near-uniform positive result tells you something important: the genre shapes the signal. Any computational method applied to interview text will run into the same issue in some form.

As you move to topic modeling, consider whether the method reveals something that sentiment cannot, or whether it runs into the same genre constraints.
